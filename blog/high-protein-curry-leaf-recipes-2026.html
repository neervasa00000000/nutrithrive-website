5 High-Protein Curry Leaf Recipes That Deliver (2026)

By Neer, NutriThrive Truganina · Last updated: 1 Aug 2026

Quick Answer

Curry leaves are not a protein source. They are the citrus-herbal flavour backbone of these five traditional high-protein dishes. Protein comes from dal, eggs, fish, or chicken—not from the leaf spoonfuls used in tempering.

Use whole curry leaves in hot oil (tadka) so the aroma blooms. Protein figures below are approximate ranges based on typical portions; recipes vary.

Key Takeaways
  • Curry leaves flavour the dish; legumes and animal proteins supply the protein.
  • Each recipe uses curry leaves differently in the tempering or simmer step.
  • Dried curry leaves work when fried in fat like fresh.
  • Pair plant dals with rice for a more complete amino acid pattern.
  • Keep a pantry pouch of shade-dried curry leaves if you cook these often.

Search results sometimes imply curry leaves are a high-protein superfood. That framing is misleading. Leaf protein by dry weight is modest, and kitchen use is a small handful or less. The dishes below are high-protein because of what else goes in the pot. Curry leaves are why those pots taste like home cooking rather than plain boiled legumes or bland sauce.

For how dals compare on protein, see which dal has the most protein.

1. Dal tadka

Soft-cooked lentils finished with a crackling tempering of mustard seeds, dried red chilli, and curry leaves. It is the weeknight protein bowl of many Indian kitchens in Australia.

Approximate protein: commonly in a mid-teens gram range per generous bowl of cooked dal before rice, depending on dry dal weight and water—use AFCD boiled lentil (~7.3 g / 100 g cooked) as a planning guide rather than a single guaranteed number.

What curry leaves do here: They go into the final tadka, not the simmer. Hot oil blooms the leaf aroma in seconds, then the tempering is poured over the dal so the scent hits first. That late addition is the signature, not simmering leaves to mush.

Practical start: 30-minute curry leaves dal.

2. Sambar

South Indian toor-based vegetable stew thickened with tamarind and spice. Protein comes mainly from toor dal plus whatever vegetables and optional add-ins are used.

Approximate protein: typically lower per bowl than dense dal tadka when vegetables stretch the pot; still a useful plant-protein meal when portions of toor are generous. Treat numbers as recipe-dependent ranges, not lab-certified per serve.

What curry leaves do here: Classic sambar tempering fries curry leaves with mustard seeds and often hing (asafoetida). The leaves cut through tamarind sourness and perfume the whole pot. Skipping them changes the dish’s identity more than swapping one vegetable.

Content gap: a dedicated NutriThrive sambar recipe post is not live yet.

3. Rasam

Thin, peppery, tamarind-forward broth often built on a little toor dal water or rasam powder. Lighter than sambar, still commonly finished with a curry-leaf tempering.

Approximate protein: usually modest unless served with substantial dal or a protein side. Think flavourful broth plus rice rather than a primary protein meal on its own.

What curry leaves do here: In rasam, leaves are fried briefly so their citrus note lifts black pepper and cumin. They are often left floating as aroma markers. Burnt leaves turn bitter fast—heat control matters more than quantity.

4. South Indian / Sri Lankan egg curry

Hard-boiled or softly simmered eggs in a coconut or onion-tomato gravy common across South India and Sri Lanka. Eggs carry most of the protein.

Approximate protein: commonly around 12–18 g from two large eggs alone, before counting any dal or yoghurt on the side—egg size and count drive the total.

What curry leaves do here: Leaves are usually tempered early with mustard seeds and onion, then stay in the gravy so the coconut or tomato base tastes herbal rather than flat. Unlike dal tadka’s last-second pour, egg curry often keeps leaves in the sauce throughout the simmer.

5. Fish or chicken moilee-style coconut curry

Kerala-style gently spiced coconut milk curry. Fish or chicken supplies the protein; the sauce is mild, fragrant, and leaf-forward.

Approximate protein: highly portion-dependent. A typical fish or chicken serve often lands in a broad ~20–35 g range for the animal protein alone when the plate is built as a main, not a tasting spoon of sauce.

What curry leaves do here: Moilee relies on curry leaves fried with ginger and green chilli in coconut oil or ghee at the start, then sometimes a second leaf tempering at the end. That double aromatic hit is the technique that separates moilee from generic coconut curry powder sauces.

Get real curry leaves for all five

Every recipe above treats curry leaves as a required aromatic, not a garnish to skip. If fresh stems are hard to find mid-week, pantry shade-dried curry leaves cover all five tempering styles—use a little more volume than fresh and fry in hot fat the same way.

FAQ

Do curry leaves have protein?

Technically a little by dry weight, practically no as eaten. Quantities are tiny. Protein in these meals comes from dal, eggs, fish, or chicken.

Can you use dried curry leaves instead of fresh?

Yes. Same ingredient. Use about 1.5× the volume of dried versus fresh, and fry in hot oil at the tempering step. See also fresh vs dried curry leaves.

What’s the difference between curry leaves and curry powder?

Curry leaves are whole leaves. Curry powder is a ground spice blend. They are not interchangeable. Full explainer: curry leaves vs curry powder.
