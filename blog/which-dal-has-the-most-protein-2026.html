Which Dal Has the Most Protein? A Ranked Guide (2026)

By Neer, NutriThrive Truganina · Last updated: 1 Aug 2026

Quick Answer

Urad dal and masoor dal usually sit at the top of raw protein rankings. Moong and toor sit a step below. Chana dal is still protein-dense, but typically a little lower per 100 g dry.

Raw figures look high because they are measured dry. Once cooked, water dilutes protein per 100 g. Food Standards Australia New Zealand’s AFCD lists dry red lentil at 23 g protein / 100 g and dried chickpea (Bengal gram) at 19.7 g / 100 g. Named urad, moong, and toor dals are not separate AFCD mature-legume entries, so those rows use commonly reported ranges.

Key Takeaways
  • Compare dals on the same basis: dry vs cooked numbers are not interchangeable.
  • AFCD (FSANZ): dry red lentil 23 g protein / 100 g; dried chickpea 19.7 g / 100 g; boiled lentils about 7.3 g / 100 g cooked.
  • Curry leaves are a tempering flavour, not a protein source.
  • Dal plus rice is a practical complete-protein meal pattern.
  • Protein targets matter more than picking one “winner” dal.

Dal is one of the most practical plant-protein staples in Australian kitchens that cook South Asian food. Rankings circulate online with confident single numbers, but sources mix raw and cooked measures and different varieties. This guide ranks common dals with that caveat front and centre, citing AFCD where a clear Australian entry exists.

Why raw and cooked protein numbers disagree

Dry dal is dense. Cooking adds water. Protein grams in the pot stay roughly the same, but protein per 100 g of the finished dish falls because the weight includes absorbed water.

That is why AFCD can list dry red lentil near 23 g protein / 100 g and boiled lentil near 7.3 g / 100 g without contradiction. Charts that mix raw and cooked values create fake winners. For meal planning, weigh dry dal before cooking, or use cooked-per-100 g figures consistently.

Curry leaf tempering (tadka): flavour, not protein

Almost every dal in this comparison is finished with a tadka: hot oil or ghee, mustard seeds, and curry leaves. That step is about aroma and taste, not macros.

Curry leaves are not a high-protein ingredient. Dried curry leaf is only a few percent protein by weight and is used in small spoonfuls. The protein in dal tadka comes from the legumes. Curry leaves are what make the tempering smell and taste like the dish people expect.

For the tempering step to work, leaves need enough heat in fat to release aroma without burning. Shade-dried whole curry leaves behave like fresh at that step when fried briefly in oil. See also the companion guide on high-protein curry leaf recipes.

Dal + rice = a complete protein pattern

Legumes are commonly lower in the amino acid methionine; grains are commonly lower in lysine. Eaten together in typical Indian meal proportions, they help cover the essential amino acids more evenly. That is a simple, shareable nutrition pattern—not a medical claim, and not a reason to ignore total daily protein from the rest of the diet.

FAQ

Is dal a complete protein?

Dal alone is typically low in methionine. Paired with rice or another grain, the amino acid profiles complement each other, which is why dal and rice is often described as a complete protein meal pattern. Completeness still depends on portions and the rest of the day’s food.

How much protein is in a bowl of dal tadka?

It depends on dal type, how many dry grams went in, how much water remains, and toppings. AFCD boiled lentil data sits around 7.3 g protein per 100 g cooked. A home bowl can often land in a mid-teens gram range before rice, but recipes vary too much for one guaranteed number.

Which dal is best for weight loss or muscle building?

No dal variety cures fat loss or builds muscle by itself. Higher-protein dals can help hit targets inside a sensible calorie and training plan. Outcomes still track total diet, training stimulus, and energy balance.
