What Does AUST L Mean on Supplements? TGA Guide Australia

AUST L is for listed medicines. Food powders sit under FSANZ. How to tell which lane applies — and what to ask for instead of a fake listing.

Quick Answer

AUST L marks a listed complementary medicine on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG). AUST R marks a registered medicine with higher-risk evaluation. Food products follow FSANZ, not AUST L. NutriThrive moringa powder is sold as a food / food supplement for kitchen use — it is not an AUST L listed medicine, and we do not invent or display an ARTG number for it. For food powders, ask for a batch lab summary or CoA; for listed medicines, verify the AUST L number on the TGA ARTG search.

Key Takeaways
  • AUST L = listed complementary medicine; sponsor certifies against TGA standards for permitted low-level claims.
  • AUST R = registered medicine; TGA evaluates quality, safety, and efficacy individually.
  • Food vs medicine are separate lanes: FSANZ for food powders; TGA/ARTG for therapeutic goods.
  • NutriThrive leaf powder is sold as food — not as an AUST L medicine. No fake listing claims.
  • When AUST L matters: listed capsules/tablets with therapeutic claims. When a CoA matters: culinary powders and food-grade greens.

Listed vs registered: the core difference

The TGA divides many complementary medicines into listed (AUST L) and registered (AUST R) pathways. Listed medicines are on the ARTG after the sponsor certifies they meet quality and safety standards and only use permitted low-level indications.

Registered medicines undergo individual TGA evaluation of quality, safety, and efficacy before approval. Higher-risk ingredients, serious claims, or novel combinations usually land here.

Primary TGA sources: the Understanding Complementary Medicines explainer (AUST L listed pathway) and the registered complementary medicines overview (AUST R). Marketing blogs should not replace reading the label and ARTG entry.

What AUST L allows and does not allow

Listed products can carry traditional or low-level claims such as supporting general health maintenance. They cannot claim to treat serious diseases unless registered with evidence.

Sponsors must hold evidence behind claims and report adverse events. Post-market review still happens. Listing is not a free pass.

If a capsule promises dramatic weight loss, cancer treatment, or hormone replacement, check whether it is appropriately registered or whether claims breach advertising rules.

Food powder vs AUST L medicines

A product is not both a generic food and a listed medicine in the same presentation. Packaging, claims, and ingredients determine classification.

FSANZ sets food standards. The TGA regulates therapeutic goods. A powder sold as a cooking ingredient follows a different framework from capsules with therapeutic claims and an AUST L number. An AUST L moringa capsule and a culinary leaf powder pouch are not regulated identically.

Honest NutriThrive position: Our shade-dried moringa powder is sold as a food / food supplement — leaf you stir into smoothies, yoghurt, soups, and meals. It is not marketed as an AUST L listed medicine. We do not invent ARTG numbers. Site legal copy is consistent: food supplements, not medicines; no TGA therapeutic claims. If someone waves a made-up “AUST L” at loose kitchen powder, treat that as a red flag.

When AUST L matters vs when a food CoA / lab summary matters

Use the right paperwork for the right lane. Mixing them up is how shoppers either over-trust a sticker or miss the documents that actually apply.

AUST L / ARTG matters when:

  • The pack is sold as a complementary medicine (often capsules or tablets).
  • The label shows an AUST L or AUST R number and makes permitted therapeutic-style indications.
  • You need to confirm the sponsor, product name, and intended use match the ARTG public summary.

A food CoA or lab summary matters when:

  • The product is sold as food or a culinary powder (ingredient list, kitchen use, no medicine number).
  • You care about batch contaminants: heavy metals, microbes, identity — not whether TGA listed it as a medicine.
  • The brand publishes a lot-matched report or summary you can open (for example an NMI lab summary), rather than vague “lab tested” wording alone.

For leaf powder, start with our heavy-metals and CoA reading guide. Pharmacy capsules often show AUST L; loose culinary powder usually will not — compare formats in powder vs capsules.

"Natural" does not mean safe

The TGA explicitly warns that natural origin does not guarantee safety or effectiveness. Complementary medicines can interact with prescriptions, affect surgery, or contain undeclared substances.

Heavy metals, microbial contamination, and wrong plant species have all triggered recalls. Natural marketing is not a quality certificate — whether the pack is listed or sold as food.

Read ingredients, check allergens, and ask your pharmacist if you take warfarin, SSRIs, blood pressure medicines, or are pregnant.

How to check the ARTG

Every listed or registered medicine displays an AUST L or AUST R number. Search that number on the TGA ARTG public search.

  1. Copy the AUST L / AUST R number from the pack (or search the brand + product name).
  2. Open the ARTG public summary and confirm product name, sponsor, and intended use match what you hold.
  3. If the pack makes medicine-style claims but shows no AUST number — and is not clearly sold as food — pause and verify before you buy.

Counterfeit labels happen online. For food powders without an AUST number, ARTG will not list them as medicines — that is expected when they are sold as food. Ask for batch lab reports instead: heavy metals, microbes, and identity testing. Our lab testing guide lists the panels worth requesting.

FAQ

Does no AUST L mean a product is unsafe?

No. Everyday foods and many culinary powders correctly carry no AUST L number because they are not listed medicines. Safety for food powder is about food standards, storage, and batch testing — not inventing a medicine listing. The risk case is different: products that make therapeutic claims without ARTG listing, or grey-market imports with neither clear food labelling nor an ARTG entry.

How do I check ARTG?

Find the AUST L or AUST R number on the pack, then search it on the TGA ARTG public summary. Confirm the name, sponsor, and intended use match. For food powders sold without an AUST number, ARTG will not show a medicine listing — use a CoA or lab summary instead. Step-by-step notes are in How to check the ARTG above.

Is NutriThrive moringa powder an AUST L medicine?

No. NutriThrive moringa powder is sold as a food / food supplement for kitchen use. It is not an AUST L listed medicine, and we do not claim an ARTG listing for the powder. Quality questions sit with published lab summaries and ordinary food labelling — not a fake AUST L sticker.

Is AUST L the same as TGA approval?

AUST L means the product is listed on the ARTG as a complementary medicine after sponsor certification against TGA standards for that pathway. It is not the same as full registered evaluation (AUST R), but it is a regulated category with obligations.

Are listed medicines safer than food powders?

Not automatically. Listed medicines have TGA oversight for their risk class. Foods follow FSANZ. Compare like with like: verify ARTG for medicines, and batch lab data for food powders. Uncategorised grey-market imports may have neither.

Does AUST L mean a product is Australian made?

No. AUST L refers to ARTG listing in Australia. Manufacturing can occur offshore if applicable rules are met. Read the label for country of origin.
