How to compare moringa brands in Australia

The Australian moringa market has grown quickly, with dozens of brands competing online, in pharmacies and in health stores. The problem with most "best brand" lists is that they award a trophy without showing the scorecard. This page uses a short, transparent checklist instead.

We sell moringa powder at NutriThrive, so we have a commercial interest in this comparison. We apply the same criteria to our product as to any other. Check our conclusions against the linked sources and product pages.

The five-point checklist

Run every brand you consider through these five questions:

  1. Lab testing: can you open a Certificate of Analysis (CoA) or lab summary that shows heavy metals and pesticide results for the actual lot you might buy? A "tested" badge with no document is not enough.
  2. Single-ingredient label: is the ingredient list 100% moringa leaf? Fillers, flavourings, and blends complicate dose maths.
  3. Processing method: shade-dried leaf tends to stay bright green. Sun-dried or heat-damaged powder often looks dull or brownish.
  4. Pack date: is there a harvest or pack date you can read? Fresher leaf retains more colour and nutrients.
  5. Price per 100g: normalise to $/100g so you can compare a 100g pouch with a 500g bag fairly.

Price: how to compare fairly

Pouches of different sizes make direct price comparison confusing. The fix is simple: divide the price by the gram count, then multiply by 100 to get $/100g.

Examples: a 500g bag at $59.95 works out to about $12/100g. A 100g bag at $11 is $11/100g. A 400g bag at $35 is $8.75/100g. The larger size is cheaper per gram, but only if you will use that much before it goes stale.

Buy quantities you will finish in three to six months, not the largest bag because the maths look good on paper. For shelf-life and storage guidance, see our moringa powder expiry guide.

Powder or capsules?

NutriThrive sells powder only. Capsules are convenient, especially for travel, but they make dose maths harder because you need to divide the mg per capsule by the number of capsules per serving, then compare that to a teaspoon of leaf powder (about 2 to 3 g, or 2,000 to 3,000 mg).

Many pharmacy-stocked capsule products give you 500 to 800 mg per serving when the label says two capsules. That is well below one teaspoon of powder. For a full format comparison, see our capsules vs powder guide.
