Capsules, tablets and "7000 mg": how to read the label

Two different things are sold under the word moringa, and the label is the only way to tell them apart before you do any price maths.

Whole-leaf powder in a capsule is dried leaf, ground, and filled into a shell. If the label says 500 mg of moringa leaf per capsule, the leaf in the bottle is the capsule count times 500 mg. That gram total is what you compare with a pouch. Format details, without a health ranking, are in moringa capsules vs powder.

A dry extract "equivalent to fresh leaf" is a different unit. On the Caruso's Moringa 7000 listing checked on 10 Oct 2026, each tablet is 1 g of dry extract equivalent to 7 g of fresh leaf. The "7000" is that fresh-leaf figure: 7 g is 7,000 mg of fresh leaf, not 7,000 mg of dried leaf powder in the tablet. Fresh leaf is mostly water. Dried leaf powder is what remains after drying. Thirty tablets times 7 g of fresh leaf is not 210 g of powder, and it is not 30 g of powder either unless the label says the extract solids weigh that much.

Before you compare a bottle with a pouch, read three lines: the ingredient (leaf or extract), the amount that belongs to the thing in the bottle, and the count. This page does not rank capsules, tablets or powder for any effect. It only lines up grams and dollars.

The maths: cost per 100g of leaf

Use one unit: dollars per 100g of leaf. A cheap-looking bottle can be a small amount of leaf. A larger pouch can cost more at the checkout and less per 100g.

Worked example for whole-leaf capsules: 120 × 500 mg = 60,000 mg. Divide by 1,000 to get grams: 60 g of leaf in the bottle. Cost per 100g = price ÷ 60 × 100.

Put a known price through that formula. Forest Super Foods' own site lists an RRP of $69 for 120 capsules at 500 mg, checked on 10 Oct 2026 at forestsuperfoods.com.au. $69 ÷ 60 × 100 = $115 per 100g. If you want powder instead

A pouch is a different label from a capsule bottle. Price per 100g does not replace a look at the pack. On any powder, check these before you pay:

  • Pack or batch date. A code on the pouch should tell you which lot you have and how to match it to paperwork. See how to read moringa batch codes and how long moringa powder lasts once the pouch is open.
  • 100% leaf. The ingredient line should say moringa leaf, or Moringa oleifera leaf, not a blend and not an extract described as fresh-leaf equivalent.
  • A lab summary you can open. Ask what was tested and for which batch. What to look for is in moringa heavy metals lab testing in Australia.
  • Origin. Where the leaf was grown, and where the pouch was packed, should be stated on the pack or the product page.
